# What Moonshot removed before the weights go out

![A large carved wooden human figure — a maker — seen three-quarters from behind, both hands resting on a partially assembled carved wooden structure that stands about waist-high.](/images/kimi-k31-pro-lead.png)

*Illustration: A maker, seen three-quarters from behind, both hands resting on a partially assembled carved wooden structure. An explanatory illustration of a model before the hand-over, not a measurement.*

By 4 November 2026, Moonshot AI has promised to hand over the files. Its new model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, has been available through the company's apps and API since 23 October, but the full weights — the files that let anyone run and modify the model on their own hardware — are promised "by Nov 4." Once files like that are widely distributed, the developer has less ability to restrict access, monitor use, or update safeguards than it does with a hosted service. That part is not controversial. It is what open weights mean.

What made this launch interesting is what Moonshot says it did first. Before letting go of the model, it says, it deliberately removed some of its cyber-offensive capability.

Two claims travelled out of the launch week. One is a leaderboard claim: a Chinese model is closing in on Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5. The other is a safety claim: a lab weakened its own model on purpose before an irreversible release. Both are worth examining — but for a reader deciding what to rely on, they are not equally load-bearing.

## The claims in the headlines

"Closing in on Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5" is [The Decoder's](https://the-decoder.com/chinese-ai-model-kimi-k3-1-pro-is-closing-in-on-anthropics-claude-opus-5-5/) framing, and it is built on one number: the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, where K3.1 Pro scores 55 — fourth place, eleven points above its predecessor Kimi K3, and three behind Claude Opus 5.5's 58. [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/23/moonshot-ai-launches-new-flagship-kim-k3-1-pro-model-with-3t-parameters/) led with scale instead: "3T parameters" in the headline. Moonshot's own phrase is "3T-class," a size category; the model has 2.8 trillion total parameters and about 104 billion active at once. The [wire story](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/moonshot-curbs-hacking-ability-in-new-kimi-model-as-fears-grow-over-aipowered-cyberattacks-4970639) that ran under "Moonshot curbs hacking ability in new Kimi model" led with the second claim.

Three outlets, three framings, one story. All of it rests on the same underlying source set: Moonshot's launch blog post and Artificial Analysis's launch analysis. That is normal for launch week — and it is exactly why the number of articles is not the number of confirmations.

## What the outside measurement shows

Artificial Analysis runs its own evaluations instead of only republishing vendor numbers, and its launch analysis is the strongest independent evidence in the set. On its Intelligence Index, K3.1 Pro gains eleven points over K3, from 44 to 55. On its agentic evaluations the gains are large: GDPval-AA, a work-task benchmark, rises 312 Elo to 1,846 — third place behind Claude Opus 5.5 (1,866) and Meta's Muse Wonder Full (1,857) — and AutomationBench-AA, which tests agentic software workflows, goes from 58% to 73%. Elsewhere the gains are more modest: Humanity's Last Exam rises from 47% to 52%.

Two cautions belong beside those numbers. The first is that a composite index is a bundle of evaluations, not a verdict on capability; the models being compared were not always run under the same settings (Claude's scores are labelled "max with fallback," Kimi's "max"). The second is that the most quoted sentence in the analysis is about the future: "Once the weights for K3.1 Pro are released it will be the highest-scoring open weights model," narrowing the gap between the best open-weights and proprietary models from twelve points to three. That is a conditional statement about an event that has not happened.

## Same benchmark, two verdicts

Here is a small puzzle from launch week that is more useful than the rankings. Terminal-Bench 4.0 tests coding agents on real tasks in a computer terminal. In Moonshot's benchmark table, K3.1 Pro scores 65.7% — ahead of GPT-6 Astra's 58.2% and, in the table's own terms, "narrowly behind Claude Opus 5.5" at 66.4%. In Artificial Analysis's own run, the same model on the same benchmark scores 67%, which the analysis calls "the highest score of any model on Terminal-Bench 4.0."

Neither number is a mistake. They are answers to different questions. Moonshot ran its model with the Claude-Code harness and took the comparison scores as "the maximum value reported in either their blog posts or the official Terminal-Bench 4.0 leaderboard." Artificial Analysis ran its own evaluations; its launch article does not state the harness or settings for this one. A reader who sees "narrowly behind" and a reader who sees "highest score of any model" are both reading the launch week accurately.

This is not a scandal. It is the normal texture of benchmark reporting, and Moonshot's footnote disclosure is better than most. But it changes how the table should be read. The comparison columns are assembled from other people's published numbers, sometimes the best of several sources; several models are run inside a rival vendor's harness — Moonshot evaluates its own model with Anthropic's Claude Code; some are graded by judge models; and in at least one case runs that retrieved answers online were scored zero. The table is a mixed document: part measurement, part compilation.

The clearest example of why this matters is the word "hallucination." Moonshot's internal Kimi Hallu Bench Hard shows the claim-level hallucination rate falling from 23.4% to 8.3%, with the company's own caveat that this is a stress test whose numbers "do not represent overall error rates in daily use." Artificial Analysis's measurement of the same behavior shows the rate falling from 53% to 44%. Both show improvement. The absolute levels differ several-fold — for the released model, 44% against 8.3% — because they are different instruments asking different questions. "The hallucination rate" is not one number.

The habit worth keeping: on launch day, ask who ran the test, with what harness, against which values of everyone else. Then decide how much weight the number can carry.

## What Moonshot says it removed

The cybersecurity section of Moonshot's launch blog is unusually specific about method, and it is the part of the story with direct consequences. During development, the company says, vulnerability-research performance kept improving as post-training scaled. Tracing the trend, it found cyber-related content embedded in the general mid-training corpus and cyber-related tasks in the reinforcement-learning environment mix — and removed data from both stages for the open-weights release, while keeping the general coding tasks.

Then it measured what that cost. xdev-bench-hard, Moonshot's in-house benchmark, is built from vulnerability discovery and exploitation tasks that reproduce recently disclosed flaws in software like the Linux kernel, Node.js, JavaScriptCore, Exim, and VirGL. The agent gets the vulnerable source code, a short hint, and a binary; it must develop an exploit, win arbitrary code execution in a separate sandbox, and retrieve a secret string to prove it. Scores are pass@3 "over a random subset of runs," with no hard timeouts or output token limits. On that benchmark, the checkpoint without the risk-reduction measures solves 90.5% of problems. The released K3.1 Pro solves 81.0%.

Read that comparison carefully, because it is both the strongest and the weakest evidence in the launch. Strongest, because a before/after against the same training pipeline is the right shape of experiment. Weakest, because every number in it is the company's own: the benchmark is in-house, the "without" checkpoint will never be released, the run subset is unspecified, and Reuters noted plainly that "the results have not been independently verified."

And the trend inside the numbers complicates the message. The released model's 81.0% is about two and a half times its predecessor's 33.3%, and Moonshot says outright that "stronger general coding and long-horizon debugging capabilities generalize to cyber tasks even after targeted data removal." On the same benchmark, DeepSeek's open model reaches 85.7%, and a third-party evaluation of GPT-5.6 Sol reached 100. The cut lowered a level. It did not remove a capability, and it did not set a ceiling. Fine-tuning on released weights can raise it again — that is structural, not a measured event, but it is what "released weights" means.

Moonshot's stated policy is narrower than the headlines suggest and worth quoting directly: "we aim not to release open weights that would extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability." The released model's offensive capability is, it says, "by design, below what our pipeline can produce," and behind frontier proprietary models that have been public for at least three months. The company intends to "progressively raise" what open models can do, arguing that open models also let more teams audit the code they maintain, including legacy systems. That is a company policy with self-defined thresholds, not an industry standard. But it is a coherent answer to a real problem.

## Why remove it before letting go?

Because letting go is the point. On the hosted API, the capability level is Moonshot's to manage — it can update, filter, or restrict. On the day the weights ship, the level is set, and everything after that belongs to whoever holds the files. A deliberate reduction is one of the few levers a lab still has before that day, and unlike a terms-of-service clause, it travels with the model itself.

The timing is also part of the story. The launch came days after China proposed export controls that would ban exports of AI-driven malware-generation and mutation technology and restrict AI-assisted vulnerability-discovery tools — draft measures still open for public comment, citing risks to critical infrastructure and government systems. A reading in which the capability cut and its published numbers are partly regulatory positioning, ahead of those rules and of Washington's scrutiny of Chinese labs, fits the same evidence as the safety reading. The company's stated reasons are on record; its motive is not inspectable, and this article will not pretend otherwise.

The scrutiny is real. In February 2026, Anthropic alleged industrial-scale distillation campaigns by DeepSeek, Moonshot, and MiniMax — over 16 million exchanges with Claude through roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts — with Moonshot's share above 3.4 million exchanges, attributed "through request metadata, which matched the public profiles of senior Moonshot staff," targeting agentic reasoning, tool use, and coding, among other capabilities. That is an accusation by an interested party, and it is not adjudicated. In July, the White House's science adviser went further, alleging the US government has information that Moonshot distilled Claude Fable 5 to build Kimi K3; the Treasury warned sanctions were "on the table"; Moonshot rejected distillation explanations and told China's National Business Daily its gains came from architecture changes, and China's commerce ministry said the accusations lack "factual or legal grounds." A targeted search found no public Moonshot response to the February post itself — a record of what we looked for, not proof of silence.

None of this decides who is right. It does show why "who controls a capability after release" is not an abstract question.

## What the release changes — and what to check

If the files arrive on 4 November, two things become readable that are not readable now. The first is whether the conditional holds — whether K3.1 Pro is in fact the highest-scoring open-weights model at release, and what that does to the twelve-point gap. The second is the license. K3.1 Pro's terms have not been published; the only guide is the license of its predecessor. The [Kimi K3 License](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3/raw/main/LICENSE) allows free use, including commercial, but requires a separate agreement with Moonshot from any "Model as a Service" business whose combined revenue exceeds $20 million over twelve months, and requires "Kimi K3" to appear in the interface of products above 100 million monthly users or $20 million monthly revenue. Internal use is exempt. Whether K3.1 Pro inherits those terms is unknown until the text exists.

Some of this matters to you only if you plan to build on the model. In that case, the useful checklist is short, and it is editorial guidance rather than a proven standard:

- **Read the license before the model.** The terms, not the benchmark table, decide what you may build.
- **Attribute numbers to their runners.** "K3.1 Pro scores 67% on Terminal-Bench 4.0" and "K3.1 Pro scores 65.7%" are both true statements about different runs.
- **Treat launch prices as a dated snapshot.** The API lists at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, about a third below K3's rates — as reported on 23 October. Artificial Analysis put the cost of one Intelligence Index task at $1.98 against K3's $2.00, as the lower prices offset 14% more output tokens.
- **If you run the weights, the cyber capability you get is a starting level, not a ceiling.**
- **Watch 4 November.** The weights, the license, and the first independent tests are the evidence that does not exist yet.

The launch established a position in a measurement: strong agentic gains on independent evaluations, a vendor table assembled with disclosed but mixed methods, and a capability reduction reported only by the company that made it. It did not establish what happens after the files are public — and the evidence that would settle that is due, like the files themselves, on November 4.
