# Mercury 3 says it is fast *and* frontier-quality. What can you verify?

On October 19, 2026, Inception released Mercury 3, the fourth model in its Mercury line and the company's flagship diffusion-based language model. The announcement made two claims together: Mercury 3 is "the most capable diffusion-based LLM released to date," and it "outperforms GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5" across a range of benchmarks while running 4–8× faster, at more than 1,000 tokens per second. The press release framed it as a new "Pareto frontier in speed and intelligence."

The very first independent checks tell a more complicated story. By October 20, the model-routing platform OpenRouter showed a live median speed of about 243 tokens per second — less than a quarter of the headline figure — with a 1.4-second wait before output even started. And Artificial Analysis, the independent benchmarking firm most often cited when models like this launch, had not published any Mercury 3 measurement at all as of October 20.

If the claim were simply "fast and cheap," it would be a small story. The claim that matters is the combination: a model that gives you frontier-level quality *and* speed at a low price would change the default math for anyone building a latency-sensitive product — a tutor that answers in a second, a browser agent that makes dozens of model calls per task, a search assistant on smart glasses. So the question is not whether Mercury 3 exists. It is what each part of the announcement actually proves, and what a reader should check before building on it.

## What Inception announced

Mercury 3 is a text-only model served through an OpenAI-compatible API, with a 1-million-token context window (up from 260,000 tokens on Mercury 2.5) and up to 131,072 output tokens. Inception says it is more than 40× larger in total parameters than its first model, Mercury 1, and that measured intelligence rose about 3× from Mercury 1 (mini) to Mercury 3. It does not publish an absolute parameter count, training details, open weights, or a technical report.

The list price is $0.20 per million input tokens and $0.70 per million output tokens; at launch the model is 50% off, at $0.10 and $0.35, with cached input tokens at $0.01 per million (90% below the input rate). There is no published end date for the promotion at the time of writing.

## What "faster" actually means

Every large language model you have used recently is autoregressive: it writes one token (roughly a word fragment) at a time, and each step depends on the ones before it. Diffusion models work differently. They start from a block of noisy, unfinished text and refine the whole block over several rounds, committing many token positions per pass. That is why diffusion models can feel fast: they move in parallel rather than one token at a time.

But "1,068 tokens per second" is a throughput number measured in a vendor test environment, not a promise about your application. Real-world speed depends on time-to-first-token, batch size, context length, the GPU behind the service, and the network. A model can decode 1,068 tokens per second on a clean GPU benchmark and still feel slow if a live user waits 1.4 seconds for the first token. The headline number and the live experience are different things.

## The benchmark table is Inception's own

Inception's launch materials include a comparison table. All three models — Mercury 3, GPT-6 Luna, and Claude Haiku 5.5 — ran at "medium" reasoning effort, and Inception ran all three itself; it does not publish the harness or sampling details.

On that table, Mercury 3 wins 8 of 12 benchmarks. The wins include instruction following (IFBench: 76.8 vs. 61.8 and 54.7), a software-engineering agent benchmark (SWE-Bench Atlas: 51.08 vs. 33.33 and 32.26), banking agent tasks (τ³-bench Banking: 30.2 vs. 23.0 and 13.1), and graduate-level science questions (GPQA Diamond: 89.2 vs. 86.4 and 84.5). The losses are telling. Claude Haiku 5.5 beats it on DeepSWE v1, a long-horizon coding benchmark (51.6 vs. 46.3). GPT-6 Luna edges it on AA-LCR v1.1, a long-context reasoning benchmark (78.3 vs. 77.7). It also trails on two more benchmarks in the table. Artificial Analysis, whose named benchmarks appear in Inception's table, cautioned that the announcement's AA-Omniscience and GDPval-AA v2 scores do not follow its methodology and are not comparable to its own scores.

Independent reporting summarized the table as "a speed story with competitive quality, not a clean sweep." That is a fair reading: Mercury 3 looks genuinely strong on instruction following and short agentic tasks, and genuinely weaker on long-context consistency and one long-horizon coding benchmark.

## What independent checks show

Three kinds of independent evidence exist as of this writing.

**Artificial Analysis has no Mercury 3 page yet.** As of October 25, its provider page still lists only Mercury 2 and 2.5, which it measures at roughly 443–464 tokens per second (Mercury 2, with about 5.6–7.8 seconds to the first token) and 714–742 tokens per second (Mercury 2.5, with about 3.6 seconds to the first token). Those are well below Inception's own figures for those models, which is a useful track record: previous Mercury models measured at roughly 60–70% of their advertised speed in independent testing. The absence of a Mercury 3 measurement means the flagship claim has not yet been checked by the most-cited independent evaluator.

**OpenRouter's live traffic is far slower than the headline.** In the week to October 20, OpenRouter's live statistics for Mercury 3 showed a median of about 243 tokens per second, a 1.40-second median latency (time to first token), and a 3.29-second median end-to-end time. This is not a like-for-like comparison — a vendor benchmark and a mixed live workload measure different things — but it is the best public picture of what actual API users are getting, and it is a large gap.

**A public community harness found real speed, competitive-but-not-frontier quality, and a strange failure mode.** An independent developer published a harness comparing Mercury 3 against the fast tiers of Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Luna, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, and — as a control — an autoregressive open model (gpt-oss-120b) hosted on Cerebras's ultra-fast hardware. On code generation (637 problems from three standard sets, five tries each), Mercury 3 at its `low` reasoning setting passed 86.0% with a 0.83-second median response. That roughly ties Claude Haiku 5.5 (86.1% at 0.97 seconds, with thinking off) and edges out GPT-6 Luna (83.2% at 1.28 seconds, with reasoning off). The Cerebras control passed 84.9% at 0.34 seconds — within about a point of Mercury, at less than half the wait. Mercury 3 decoded about 850–900 visible tokens per second in the tester's setup — about 6× GPT-6 Luna, but about 2.7× *slower* than the Cerebras-hosted autoregressive model. The tester's verdict: the speed is real, but it is a product attribute (model plus serving stack), not a property of diffusion alone.

In the same harness, a SQL agent loop (150 questions, up to eight tool-use turns) told a richer story. Mercury 3 finished tasks at a 2.05-second median — 1.9–2.8× faster than Haiku (3.87 s), Gemini (4.44 s), and Luna (5.69 s) — even though Claude Haiku was about four to five points more accurate on the same questions and made fewer calls. In other words: per-call speed compounds in agent loops, but a faster model that needs more turns or gets more answers wrong does not automatically win.

The harness also found a failure mode worth knowing about. On code tasks, 7.5–8.6% of Mercury 3's calls "reasoned silently" until the 32,768-token cap and returned nothing — consuming 61% of the model's output tokens in that test. No other tested model did this even once. For a real application, that means a portion of requests can come back empty after burning your token budget, and you need retry logic or a timeout.

## The price picture

At the launch price, Mercury 3 is cheap: $0.10 per million input and $0.35 per million output tokens, with $0.01 cached input, and 100 million free tokens for new accounts with no payment details. But per-token price is not per-answer cost. The same community harness computed that at the promo price, one thousand passing code answers on Mercury 3 cost about $0.59 — versus $0.07 for GPT-6 Luna with reasoning off, which was nearly as accurate and more than 1.5× faster on that task. In the agent task, Mercury 3 cost about $1.38 per thousand correct tasks at promo prices, versus $0.77 for Luna; at regular prices, Claude Haiku was cheaper than every Mercury setting. The silent-thinking failures add to real cost: wasted output tokens that produce no answer.

So "cheap per token" is real, and "cheap per correct answer" depends on your workload, your reasoning setting, and whether you are on promo pricing.

## A real deadline for existing Inception customers

Alongside the launch, Inception retired its older models for new users: Mercury 2 and Mercury 2.5 are no longer available to new sign-ups, and existing customers with usage before October 19, 2026 keep access only until **November 15, 2026** (enterprise customers remain supported under their agreements). If you already run Mercury 2 or 2.5 in production, you have a migration decision on a fixed calendar — and the natural replacement, Mercury 3, is a different model with different pricing, output limits, and failure behavior, not a drop-in with identical behavior.

## What you can conclude — and what you should still check

What is solidly documented: Mercury 3 exists, costs what Inception lists (for now), has a 1M-token context, an OpenAI-compatible API, a 100M-token free tier, and a November 15 migration deadline for Mercury 2/2.5 users. What is vendor-claimed and not yet independently confirmed: frontier-level quality parity with GPT-6 Luna and Haiku 5.5, the 1,068 tokens-per-second figure, and the "diffusion scales" framing. What independent checks suggest: the speed is real in parallelizable tasks and agent loops, but live users see far lower throughput than the headline; quality is competitive but not a clean sweep, with clear weaknesses in long-context consistency and one long-horizon coding benchmark; and the model has a documented silent-failure mode.

For a latency-sensitive agent loop with short outputs, Mercury 3 at promo prices is a genuinely interesting candidate — but run your own evaluation on your own tasks before trusting it, budget for empty responses, and plan for prices to double when the promotion ends. For quality-critical, long-output, or long-context work, the honest position today is: wait for independent evaluations (Artificial Analysis has not measured the model yet) and do not treat the launch table as proof.
