# What does an AI lab's compute bill commit it to?

![The Orange Tomato mascot stands before a graffiti wall at night, its chest screen showing the article title; the wall is sprayed with promised, spent, planned, assumed, and filing.](\/images\/anthropic-compute-commitments-lead.png)

Three phrases in one filing describe three different promises: "at least $110.0 billion," "up to $120.5 billion," and "approximately $417 billion."

They all appear in [Anthropic's S-1, filed October 26, 2026 and amended November 2, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026068676/anthropicpbc-sx1ax1.htm). The headlines that followed mostly collapsed them into a single number — the compute bill — and set it next to another number, the valuation. That comparison misses the structure that matters. The useful question is not how big the total sounds. It is which dollars are firmly promised, which depend on delivery, which are only estimates, and which have not been spent at all.

This is a filing explainer, not an investment case. We read the prospectus as filed; we did not test systems, audit data centers, or model the company's finances.

## What the filing says it earned

In 2023, 2024, and 2025, revenue was $41 million, $386 million, and $4.6 billion, respectively. In the nine months ended September 30, 2026, revenue was $33.2 billion, up from $2.5 billion a year earlier — the third quarter alone was $17.0 billion, up from $1.4 billion. The amended filing reports year-over-year growth of 841% in 2024, 1,088% in 2025, and 1,206% on that nine-month figure — issuer-computed rates we have not yet recomputed from statement inputs.

The same paragraph refuses to let those rates travel forward. Recent revenue growth and financial performance "should not be considered indicative of our future performance." The company says it does not expect revenue to keep growing at comparable rates, and cannot assure it will keep growing at all.

That caution matters because the filing is, by design, a selling document from an interested party. Fast growth from a small base, early adoption dynamics, and a rapidly expanding customer count can produce rates that arithmetic alone will pull down as the base grows. The filing names that mechanism rather than leaving readers to discover it. Any reading that extends 1,206% forward is arguing against the document it cites.

Operating loss was $1.3 billion for the nine months, down from $5.2 billion a year earlier; the company also reports Adjusted Operating Income of $4.1 billion against an Adjusted Operating Loss of $3.1 billion — a company-defined non-GAAP measure. Treat full profitability as still in transition in this article: the direction improved, the basis is the issuer's.

## What the filing says it committed

After December 31, 2025, the company entered into new and amended existing lease and commercial agreements "primarily to secure additional compute and infrastructure capacity, with aggregate future minimum payment obligations of approximately $417 billion." Those consist primarily of compute-equipment and data-center leases plus cloud purchase commitments, starting between 2026 and 2027, with terms from under a year to 20 years.

Two named pieces sit inside that broader program, and they work differently.

In April 2026, Anthropic committed to Amazon an aggregate of "at least $110.0 billion" between May 2026 and April 2036, "regardless of actual usage." If spend falls short, Anthropic pays the difference as a true-up, subject to adjustments and exceptions. The commitment is non-cancelable except on Amazon's uncured material breach. Read plainly: this is the closest thing in the filing to a take-or-pay promise. The exact adjustments live in the exhibit, which we have not yet verified line by line.

Between May and October 2026, Anthropic entered and amended several agreements with SpaceX.AI LLC — SpaceXAI — for NVIDIA-GPU-backed compute. There it "agreed to spend an aggregate of up to $120.5 billion over terms extending into 2029," at roughly $3.1 billion per month "once all capacity has been delivered, which we expect in December 2026," with different fees while capacity ramps. After initial non-cancelable periods ending in January and March 2027, either party can cancel on 90 days' notice.

"At least" and "up to" are doing opposite work. One sets a floor that survives low usage. The other sets a ceiling that depends on delivery and survives only about a year in non-cancelable form. A separate AMD hardware-supply deal underscores the range: it names no fixed dollar commitment at all, though the filing says procuring the full initial tranche would be expected to exceed $20.0 billion. An article that adds these into a single obligation total is manufacturing certainty the filing withholds.

A third number must stay out of any sum. The company also expanded agreements for capacity to be delivered between 2028 and 2031, estimating "$125 billion to $150 billion" in future payments — which the filing states are "not included in the contractual commitments table above" because commercial terms are not finalized. An estimate with open terms is planning information, not a commitment.

## Committed, spent, planned, assumed

![Chalkboard diagram inside the outline of the Orange Tomato mascot head showing the filing's four buckets: signed minimums, cash spent and expense, planned estimates, and assumed future revenue, with the filed figures in their buckets.](\/diagrams\/anthropic-compute-commitments-buckets.svg)

*Diagram: the filing's four buckets — signed minimums (~$417B), Amazon floor (≥$110B) and SpaceXAI cap (≤$120.5B), excluded plan ($125–150B), and assumed revenue ($33.2B 9M with warning); cover sets no valuation.*

Four buckets keep the filing straight:

**1. Signed minimums.** The ~$417 billion in future minimum payment obligations. This is what the contracts require at minimum if their terms run — not cash already paid, not expense already recognized, not capital expenditure already incurred. Leases, purchase commitments, and take-or-pay true-ups belong here, each with its own conditions.

**2. Cash spent and expense recognized.** A separate question the commitments table does not answer by itself: how much has left the door, and how much hit the income statement. The filing distinguishes commitments that "generally extend over multiple years" from the timing of delivery and payment, and warns about committed outflows that do not produce immediate revenue and about being committed to excess capacity if needs change. Exact next-twelve-months and cash-flow figures are held for the statements check — this article teaches the category without inventing its number.

**3. Planned and estimated.** The $125–150 billion for 2028–2031 delivery. The filing labels this an estimate with terms to be finalized and keeps it out of its own table. It tells you where management expects to go next, not what it owes today.

**4. Still assumed.** The future revenue that would cover buckets 1–3. The filing's $33.2 billion nine-month figure is real and filed; its continuation at anything like 1,206% is explicitly disclaimed. The gap between a minimum obligation and the revenue that services it is an assumption, and the filing marks it as one.

Headline valuation talk belongs in none of these buckets. The S-1 cover as filed shows no public market and a blank price range — "between $ and $." Reported targets of up to $100 billion raised at $1.5–2 trillion, aiming at the week of November 16, are wire-reported figures, not filed ones. A valuation follows from bucket 4. It does not restate buckets 1–3.

## What has to go right — and what the filing says could go wrong

For the obligations to look prudent in hindsight, three things must hold: capacity must arrive roughly as expected (notably the SpaceXAI December 2026 delivery expectation); utilization must be high enough that take-or-pay floors like Amazon's do not become payments for idle capacity; and revenue must keep growing fast enough — though not at 1,206% — to service multi-year minimums while the company absorbs ramping fees and build-out costs.

The filing lists the failure modes without euphemism: markets developing more slowly than expected, growth slowing or stalling, being "committed to excess compute that remains underutilized," and "committed cash outflows that do not produce immediate revenue" when compute timing and demand timing misalign. Those are standard cloud-commitment risks stated at unusual scale — not evidence they will happen, but the document's own boundary on optimism.

One conspicuously absent corroboration: we have not yet verified counterparty disclosures (Amazon) or the full exhibit terms behind the true-up, ramping fees, and deployment conditions. SpaceX is private; no matching public disclosure should be assumed to exist. Until those are checked, every obligation sentence above rests on the issuer's description alone.

## How to read the next headline

All figures here are per the November 2, 2026 amendment (S-1/A), extracted the same day. The November listing peg is reported timing, not a filed commitment; if it slips, the peg goes with it rather than stretching the piece.

When the next "lab commits $X billion" headline arrives, three questions recover most of what this filing exercise teaches. Is the number "at least," "up to," "approximately," or an estimate — a floor, a ceiling, a rounded minimum, or a plan? Does it describe a commitment, cash paid, or expense recognized — an obligation, an outflow, or an accounting charge? And what revenue assumption closes the gap between the promise and the payment — stated in the document, or supplied by the headline?

Anthropic's filing, read on its own terms, does not answer whether $417 billion of minimums plus a ten-year Amazon floor plus a cancelable GPU ceiling is wise. It answers the narrower question investors actually need first: which of those numbers is which kind of promise, and which parts of the story the document refuses to guarantee.
